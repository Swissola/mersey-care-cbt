## Part 1 — Behavioural Activation & Values

### Why Activity Matters

When people feel depressed they often feel physically unwell, have negative thoughts, and experience a lack of energy and motivation. As a result, they do less. The trouble is, doing less makes people feel worse — creating a self-reinforcing cycle:

```text
Feel low → Do less → Feel worse → Do even less → Feel even worse
```

**Addressing the reduction in activity is one of the most important things you can do when tackling depression.** Re-introducing activities — even small ones — begins to break this cycle from the bottom up.

**Spoon Theory**

A helpful way to think about energy when you are depressed is Spoon Theory. Imagine you start each day with a limited number of spoons — each one representing a unit of energy. Every activity you do costs spoons. When you are well, you might start the day with fifteen spoons and cooking dinner costs one. When you are depressed, you might start with five spoons and cooking dinner costs three. You run out faster, and recovery takes longer.

This is why pacing matters. It is not laziness — it is managing a genuinely limited resource. The goal of behavioural activation is to gradually increase the number of spoons you start with, by rebuilding activity slowly and consistently.

### The Problem with Avoidance

Avoidance is understandable — it requires little effort and the short-term relief is immediate. But in the long term, avoidance:

- Maintains feelings of low mood and anxiety
- Prevents you from learning that you **can** cope
- Means things don't turn out as badly as feared
- Causes you to miss out on potentially rewarding and enjoyable experiences

**Three types of activities people commonly start to avoid:**

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Routine** | Cooking, housework, laundry, getting up and dressed, everyday hygiene |
| **Pleasurable** | Going out with friends, walking, gardening, reading, listening to music |
| **Necessary** | Attending appointments, food shopping, replying to messages or emails, paying bills |

### Task 2: Activity Ranking

Depression shifts your sense of what is manageable. Something that once felt effortless — cooking a meal, going for a walk — can now feel like a significant effort. That is not weakness, it is the illness. This ranking helps you see your activities honestly, so you can plan a gradual return rather than overwhelming yourself by starting too high.

Your ranking should also reflect your personal circumstances — your physical ability, mobility, health conditions, and energy levels. An activity that is easy for someone else may be genuinely hard for you, and that is completely valid. Rate things as they are for you, not as they are for anyone else.

### Finding Your Values

Values are the things that are truly important to you in life. They are not goals to be ticked off, and they are not a list of "shoulds" from other people. They come from your own sense of what enriches your life and gives you a sense of self-respect.

Values are like compass directions — they give purpose and meaning, and help you find your way when depression has knocked you off course.

**Reflection questions:**

- **Passions** — What am I passionate about? What do I love doing? What could I not live without?
- **Skills** — What am I good at? What do I want to get better at?
- **What matters** — What is important to me? Being a good parent? Earning a living? Faith? Something else?
- **Legacy** — What do I want to be remembered for?

## Part 2 — Relaxation & Breathing

### Why Relaxation Helps

When tense, you experience uncomfortable physical symptoms — headaches, backaches, and so on. These physical sensations add to your mental worry. Relaxation works by:

- Slowing down the body systems that speed up during anxiety
- Reducing the physical discomfort that feeds worry
- Improving your capacity to cope with daily stresses
- Improving quality of life over time

---

### Breathing Techniques

**Test your breathing first:**

1. Lie down for a few minutes
2. Place one hand on your stomach, one on your chest
3. Breathe normally and notice which hand moves more

If the hand on your **chest** moves more, you are not breathing diaphragmatically. Correct breathing has your stomach moving slightly more than your chest.

#### Diaphragmatic Breathing

> 📝 *Practise daily until this becomes your natural breathing pattern.*

- [ ] Place one hand on your stomach, one on your chest
- [ ] Breathe slowly and regularly for a few minutes
- [ ] Gradually get the hand on your stomach to move more than the hand on your chest
- [ ] Some muscle strain is normal at first — the diaphragm may not have been properly exercised

---

#### Deep Breathing

> 📝 *Practise this exercise daily.*

- [ ] Take a complete breath in, filling every part of the lungs
- [ ] Breathe in slowly and deeply, then pause for a moment
- [ ] Let the breath out slowly
- [ ] Pause again, then repeat
- [ ] Do this several times, as slowly as possible

---

#### Rhythmic Breathing

> 📝 *Aim for approximately 5 minutes of practice.*

- [ ] Place hands on stomach with fingertips touching
- [ ] Breathe in through the nose, counting to six
- [ ] Breathe out, counting to six
- [ ] Notice fingertips moving apart and coming together — this is your diaphragm working correctly
- [ ] Aim for around five breaths per minute

---

#### Box Breathing

> 📝 *Use this when you need to calm down quickly — during anxious moments, before a difficult situation, or when your mind is racing.*

- [ ] Breathe in slowly through your nose, counting to four
- [ ] Hold the breath for a count of four
- [ ] Breathe out slowly through your mouth, counting to four
- [ ] Hold for a count of four
- [ ] Repeat four times

> 💡 *Visualise drawing the four sides of a box as you go — up as you breathe in, across as you hold, down as you breathe out, across as you hold again. This gives your mind something to focus on and helps interrupt the anxiety response.*
