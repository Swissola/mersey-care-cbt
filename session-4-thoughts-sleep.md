## Part 1 — Challenging Unhelpful Thoughts

### Negative Thinking Styles

<details open>
<summary>Common unhelpful thinking patterns — click to expand</summary>

Everyone has automatic thinking styles — mental shortcuts the brain uses as part of its "safety first" approach. The problem is that these shortcuts can lead to unhelpful conclusions that make anxiety and depression worse.

| Thinking Style | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **All-or-nothing thinking** | Evaluating yourself in black and white terms | *"I did that badly — I might as well not bother."* |
| **Overgeneralisation** | Seeing one negative event as a never-ending pattern; using "always", "never", "everyone", "nothing" | *"I had to leave the restaurant. I'll never cope with eating in public."* |
| **Catastrophising** | Exaggerating the importance of mistakes or fears out of all proportion | *"I made a fool of myself in the supermarket — I'll never live it down."* |
| **Emotional reasoning** | Assuming your negative emotions reflect reality | *"I feel like a failure, therefore I am a failure."* |
| **Personalisation** | Taking all responsibility for negative events with no real basis | *"It's my fault the party was a flop because I didn't speak to enough people."* |
| **Mental filter** | Picking out one negative detail and dwelling on it exclusively | *"I'm a bad parent because I forgot to pick up my son this afternoon."* |
| **Labelling / mislabelling** | Attaching a harsh negative label to yourself after a mistake | *"I am an idiot."* |
| **Disqualifying the positive** | Dismissing successes as luck or coincidence | *"My boss said my work was good — they must have felt sorry for me."* |
| **Jumping to conclusions** | Assuming someone is reacting negatively, or predicting bad outcomes | *"She didn't smile at me today — I must have done something wrong."* |
| **Should statements** | Using "should" and "ought" as motivation, leading to guilt when not met | *"I should have finished everything I planned to do by now."* |

</details>

### Why Challenge Your Thoughts?

<details open>
<summary>The reasoning behind thought challenging — click to expand</summary>

In the example below, the **situation** is identical. What differs is the **thought** — and that single difference changes everything that follows.

| | Situation | Thought | Feelings | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Hear a noise at night | *"I'm being burgled!"* (Catastrophising) | Panic, fear, worry | Call police; stay awake all night |
| **B** | Hear a noise at night | *"That must be the cat outside."* | Fine, neutral | Look out the window and go back to bed |

If we are able to think about our thoughts more rationally — using evidence rather than automatic shortcuts — we can directly change how we feel and behave.

</details>

---

### The 4-Step Thought Challenging Process

<details open>
<summary>How to challenge a thought — click to expand</summary>

**Step 1 — Notice and label**
Pause and identify the thought. What is the situation? Where are you? Who are you with? How are you feeling because of this thought?

**Step 2 — Gather evidence**
Think of all the evidence **for** and **against** your negative thought. Write them in two columns. Ask: *"If I had to present this evidence in court, would it stand up?"*

**Step 3 — Create a balanced alternative**
Review the original thought in light of the evidence. Can it be reframed into a more accurate, balanced version?

**Step 4 — Notice the change**
How do you feel now, thinking the more balanced thought?

</details>

## Part 2 — Sleep Hygiene

### Sleep Stages

<details open>
<summary>Understanding the sleep cycle — click to expand</summary>

During sleep, the body cycles through distinct stages. Getting good quality sleep means allowing the body to move through all of them.

| Stage | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1** | Transition | The boundary between awake and asleep — not quite one or the other |
| **Stage 2** | True sleep | Properly asleep but not yet deeply; can still be woken fairly easily |
| **Stages 3 & 4** | Deep sleep | Takes around 30 minutes to reach the deepest point; most physically restorative |
| **Stage 5** | REM sleep | Rapid Eye Movement — where dreaming happens; important for emotional processing |

</details>

---

### 5 Bad Habits of Sleep

<details open>
<summary>Common sleep disruptors — click to expand</summary>

Research identifies five key areas that commonly disrupt sleep. Addressing these first — before trying more complex interventions — is the recommended starting point.

**Caffeine**
- A stimulant that keeps the body awake
- Found in tea, coffee, chocolate, most fizzy drinks and energy drinks
- Avoid caffeine at least 4 hours before bed
- Recommended daily limit: ~300mg (approximately 3 cups of coffee or 4 cups of tea)

**Nicotine**
- Also a stimulant — avoid smoking within an hour of bed
- Do not smoke if you wake in the night — it disrupts return to sleep and trains the body to wake craving it

**Alcohol**
- A common myth is that alcohol helps sleep — it doesn't
- Alcohol has a sedative effect initially, but disrupts sleep as it is processed by the body during the night
- Results in waking, needing the toilet, needing water, and lower mood the next morning

**Diet**
- Avoid large meals close to bedtime — the body stays active digesting food
- A small snack (e.g. toast) can help some people

**Exercise**
- Exercise during the day helps with sleep at night by releasing stress and worry
- However, avoid vigorous exercise within 2 hours of bed — the endorphin release is energising

</details>

### Bedroom Factors

<details open>
<summary>Environment and sleep quality — click to expand</summary>

The environment in which you sleep has a significant impact on sleep quality. Consider these four factors:

**Noise**
- People with insomnia are often more sensitive to noise
- Strategies: close windows, use earplugs, move to a quieter room

**Light**
- The body releases melatonin (the sleep hormone) when it gets dark — and stops when it gets light
- Maximise light during the day; minimise it at night
- Reduce light from TVs, phones, and street lighting

**Comfort**
- A tidy, uncluttered room feels more relaxing
- Making your bed each morning makes it a nicer place to return to
- Mattresses are designed to last approximately 7 years — flip or rotate regularly

**Temperature**
- Rooms above 24°C are associated with reduced deep sleep and increased night waking
- Rooms below 12°C are associated with emotional and broken sleep
- Find what works for you within this range

</details>

### 5 Good Habits of Sleep

<details open>
<summary>Building a healthy sleep routine — click to expand</summary>

**1. Bedtime routine**
Around an hour before bed, wind down with something enjoyable and calming — but do this in a room other than the bedroom, to maintain the association between bedroom and sleep.

**2. Reduce napping**
Napping can reduce sleep drive and quality at night. If you must nap, keep it to no more than 40 minutes and before 3pm.

**3. Bedroom for sleep only**
Watching TV, using your phone, eating, or working in bed trains your brain to associate the bedroom with being awake. The bedroom should signal sleep.

**4. Only go to bed when sleepy**
There is a difference between being *tired* (exhausted) and *sleepy* (ready to sleep). Wait for sleepiness. Going to bed too early gives more time to lie awake worrying.

**5. The 20-minute rule**
If you have not fallen asleep after about 20 minutes, get up. Go to another room. Do something quiet. Only return to bed when you feel sleepy again.

</details>

### Sleep Retraining

<details open>
<summary>A hard reset for persistent insomnia — click to expand</summary>

If good habits alone are not enough, sleep retraining works by rebuilding the body's natural sleep drive. It is the equivalent of a hard reset.

**The three rules:**

1. **Only go to bed when sleepy** — the longer you stay awake, the greater the pressure to sleep. Use this in your favour.

2. **The 20-minute rule** — if you can't sleep after ~20 minutes, get up and do something quiet in another room. Return only when sleepy. Repeat as needed.

3. **Get up at your normal time regardless** — no matter how many times you were up in the night. Do not nap during the day. Let sleep pressure build so the following night is easier.

> ⚠️ This process can feel difficult at first and may involve a few difficult nights. This is normal and expected. The cumulative sleep pressure means improvement typically follows within a week or two.

</details>

### Coping Strategies for Sleep

<details open>
<summary>Techniques to help a restless mind — click to expand</summary>

**Safe Place Imagery**
Create a mental story about somewhere you feel completely safe, happy, and at ease. It can be a real place, a remembered holiday, or somewhere entirely imagined. Build it in detail — what you see, hear, feel, smell.

**Putting the Day to Rest**
Set aside 20 minutes in the evening to write down anything you need to remember for tomorrow. Once written, remind yourself it is dealt with — then let it go when you get into bed.

**Thought Blocking**
Choose a single neutral word (traditionally "the") and silently mouth it repeatedly for a few minutes. This aims to crowd out racing thoughts and reduce the mental activity that prevents sleep.

</details>
